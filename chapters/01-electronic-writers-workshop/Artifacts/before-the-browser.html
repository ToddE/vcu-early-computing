VCU LIBRARIES & COLLEGE OF HUMANITIES AND SCIENCES /// archival referral, prepared by A. Todd Emerson

Before the Browser

How an undergraduate, an English professor, and a campus systems consultant built VCU's first networked writing classroom — three years before anyone on campus had seen a web page.

Account has0ate, Cabell/Hibbs DEC UNIX Active 1990–1993 Department English, VCU

In a home directory recovered from a decommissioned VCU DEC system sits the working record of SHARE, later formalized as the Electronic Writers’ Workshop — a menu-driven program that let students submit, exchange, and comment on each other’s papers over the campus network. It ran on text terminals. It predates the graphical web. And it was built by a VCU undergraduate under a state grant, with an English professor as the client and a campus systems administrator as co-developer.

01What was actually built

Starting around 1990, Todd Emerson — then a VCU English undergraduate — received an Undergraduate Research Grant for a project titled Computers and Prewriting (later Computers and Freewriting), supervised by Dr. Ann M. Woodlief. The original goal was narrow: a Pascal program that would prompt students through a prewriting exercise. What it grew into was broader — a full classroom file-exchange system, built in c-shell on VCU’s networked DEC machines.

The system, SHARE, gave students a text menu with three real actions: grab a file from a shared class folder, share a file of their own into it, or submit a paper directly to their professor’s box. It was wired into WordPerfect 5.0 through a keyboard macro, so a student could go from writing to submitting without leaving the word processor.

EWW user manual — macro invocation
hibbs% wp50
PF3 KP2
Macro: share
… Grab & Share menu appears

By 1992–93 the system had been packaged and formally copyrighted as the Electronic Writers’ Workshop, complete with an installation script, a numbered-folder system for organizing multiple class sections, and a written manual. It was in real use: Dr. Woodlief ran her own class through it, posting discussion questions to a shared folder that students would “grab,” annotate, and turn back in as reading responses — a workflow described plainly, in the collection’s own words, as follows.

STAFF.INF — internal description, ca. 1992–93on classroom use
“Dr. Anne Woodlief has the program set up to store a series of questions or things to think about while reading different texts. The students are able to ‘grab’ and edit electronic copies of the questions… and store their reading responses on their private account.”

02How it happened

1990–91
Emerson receives an Undergraduate Research Grant for a computer-assisted prewriting heuristic. Early prototype written in Pascal, intended to run on VCU’s UNIX network.
1991
Michael A. Keller, Computer Coordinator of the VCU Writing/Computer Center, becomes Emerson’s working partner and grant sponsor. Together they also build TUTOR, a c-shell wrapper that simplified the vi editor for students, and help select hardware (386-33 PCs with MS-Windows) for the English Department.
Oct 1992
Emerson requests a disk-quota increase from ACCS (Academic Campus Computing Services) to continue development under a new SCHEV grant, citing four years of accumulated coursework and program files. Confirmed in a reply from ACCS’s Steven Saltzberg, then attending the national EDUCOM conference.
Oct 1992–Jun 1993
The bulk of the surviving correspondence: 139 email exchanges with Dr. Woodlief covering folder structures, student access, and day-to-day troubleshooting of what she calls, in one subject line, “The Electronic Workshop.”
Nov 1992
Gregory S. DeBruyn, VCU’s systems consultant (“SC Mgr”), begins contributing directly to the codebase — teaching Emerson ANSI terminal escape codes for the interface’s inverse-video menus, and sharing a reusable file-management script.
Feb 15–16, 1993
Dr. Woodlief writes directly to Steven A. Saltzberg of ACCS, calling the program “totally unique” and urging VCU to formally recognize and protect Emerson’s work before he graduates. Saltzberg replies cautiously, raising questions of ownership and noting ACCS’s limited resources to adopt or support it — the first documented moment the software is discussed at an administrative level.
Feb 25, 1993
Emerson demonstrates SHARE to the VCU Faculty User Group (FUG) in Cabell Hall, at the invitation of committee chair Don Forsyth — an audience of faculty, administrators, and computing staff that included Saltzberg, gathered that month specifically to discuss “using email in teaching.”
1993
SHARE is repackaged as the copyrighted Electronic Writers’ Workshop, with a formal manual and installation process, for continued use by English Department faculty.

—A thread worth pulling

Steven Saltzberg, the ACCS administrator who fielded Emerson’s quota requests and sat in the room for the Feb 1993 FUG demonstration, later co-created Web Course in a Box — an early web-based course management system built at VCU that was licensed nationally in the mid-to-late 1990s — and went on to co-found MadDuck Technologies. Nothing in this collection proves a direct line from SHARE to that later work. But the documentary trail is real: Saltzberg personally administered Emerson’s account, was lobbied in writing on the significance of what Emerson had built, and watched it demonstrated to VCU’s assembled academic-computing leadership two years before the web browser existed on most desktops. That coincidence of person, place, and subject seems worth a researcher’s attention — and Michael Keller’s own surviving correspondence from this period, not yet located, may hold the rest of the story.

AWOOD — Ann M. Woodlief to Steven Saltzberg, Feb 15, 1993on Todd's software
“Todd has developed this fantastic share-and-grab program for WP50 which seems to be totally unique—nothing quite like it seems to be available, not even for big bucks… This could potentially be a very big deal, and I want him to benefit from it as much as possible.”

03The people

Four names recur across the source code and the mail. None of them thought they were making institutional history — the tone throughout is collegial, a little tired, occasionally joking. That ordinariness is part of what makes the record valuable.

Dr. Ann M. Woodlief

English Department — grant supervisor, end user

Client and champion. Her mailbox with Emerson is the largest single document in the collection — the day-to-day record of a professor actually running a class through software an undergraduate was still debugging.

Michael A. Keller

Computer Coordinator, Writing/Computer Center

Grant co-sponsor and technical mentor. Co-built TUTOR (the vi wrapper) and advised on department hardware purchasing decisions.

Gregory S. DeBruyn

Systems consultant (“SC Mgr”)

Contributed terminal-programming know-how directly into SHARE’s interface code, including the escape sequences behind its inverse-video menu highlighting.

Steven A. Saltzberg

Academic Campus Computing Services

Approved Emerson's disk-quota extension, fielded Woodlief's appeal to formally recognize the software, and attended its Feb 1993 demonstration to the Faculty User Group. Later co-created Web Course in a Box and MadDuck Technologies — see below.

Don Forsyth & Kenneth C. Guyre

FUG chair; ACCS Manager of Technical Services

Forsyth invited Emerson to demonstrate SHARE to the Faculty User Group; Guyre processed the account changes that kept the project’s disk quota — and Emerson himself — on VCU's systems.

05Why it belongs in an archive

—

It predates the web at VCU

SHARE and EWW were built and in classroom use years before browser-based tools existed on campus. This is a homegrown learning-management system, built from scratch, in a period usually documented (if at all) only by institutional memoranda — not by the working code and correspondence itself.

—

It documents state-funded innovation from the ground up

The collection traces a SCHEV-funded project from grant proposal through working deployment, including the administrative correspondence (quota requests, ACCS approvals) that rarely survives alongside the technical output it enabled.

—

It's a cross-role collaboration record

Faculty, student, and IT staff appear together as co-developers, not as separate constituencies — a structure of collaboration that VCU's current teaching-technology initiatives still aim for.

—

The record is unusually complete

Two independent backups preserved not just the finished software but its drafts, its bug reports, and the email trail of decisions behind it — source code and provenance together, which is rare for informal projects of this era.

06What's in the collection

Sixty-one files, recovered from two backup copies of the original DEC UNIX account, organized into four series. A full item-level finding aid accompanies this overview. Email addresses, phone numbers, and personal-activity content unrelated to the project have been redacted or removed.

SERIES 1
SHARE System Source

The original c-shell menu program — grab, share, submit, exit — and its successive backups and utility scripts.

SERIES 2
EWW System Documentation

The packaged, copyrighted release: manual, setup script, folder-numbering system, and the internal description of Dr. Woodlief's actual classroom workflow.

SERIES 3
Grant & Research Documentation

The SCHEV quota letter, grant proposal and progress reports, and the original Pascal prototype of the prewriting program.

SERIES 4
Development Correspondence

The Woodlief mailbox in full, plus correspondence with Keller, DeBruyn, and VCU's Hibbs site manager — the working conversation behind the code.

Prepared from the has0ate DEC UNIX home directory, VCU Cabell/Hibbs systems, recovered via two independent backup copies.
